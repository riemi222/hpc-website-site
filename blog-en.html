From New Chitose Airport to Furano Ski Resort, the fastest JR route takes about 3 hours (Rapid Airport train to Sapporo Station, then transfer to the Furano Lavender Express limited express). There's also a winter-only direct bus (December through April) running about 6 times a day for roughly 4,500-5,000 yen. Asahikawa can be reached via airport highway bus in about 1.5-2 hours. If you're carrying heavy ski gear and want to skip timetables and transfers, HPC offers direct transfer service from New Chitose Airport to Furano and Asahikawa resorts, door to door.

How many transfers does the JR route to Furano require, and how long does it take?

The standard JR route from New Chitose Airport starts with the Rapid Airport train to Sapporo Station, then a transfer to the Furano Lavender Express limited express train, which runs direct to Furano. Including the transfer, the whole trip takes about 3 hours. This route runs on a fixed, predictable schedule, which suits light-packing independent travelers, but hauling snowboard and boot bags between platforms and up and down stairs in winter isn't easy for first-time visitors to Hokkaido — especially if a delayed train throws off your connection and you end up waiting around at the station.

Is the winter-only direct bus worth it? What about schedule and booking?

From December through April, New Chitose Airport runs a seasonal direct bus to Furano, about 6 departures a day, priced roughly 4,500-5,000 yen — skipping the hassle of hauling gear on and off train cars. The earlier you plan your trip, the earlier you should lock in your bus seat or transfer vehicle, so you're not caught off guard finding the day's departures already full.

How do you get from New Chitose Airport to Asahikawa? What's the difference between a private transfer and the bus?

From New Chitose Airport to central Asahikawa, you can take the airport highway bus (about 1.5-2 hours, with more frequent departures than the Furano bus) or a JR limited express in a similar timeframe. Do you need a Chinese-speaking coach to ski in Hokkaido, and how much does an advanced ski course cost?

You don't strictly need a coach to ski in Hokkaido, but for beginners or anyone looking to improve technique, a Chinese-speaking coach shortens the learning curve significantly and adds a layer of safety. Chinese-speaking coaches are mainly available at Niseko, Rusutsu, Furano, Sapporo Teine, Sapporo Kokusai, and Hoshino Resorts Tomamu, with full-day lessons running roughly NTD14,500-18,000 and half-day around NTD11,500-13,500 depending on coach level and group size, while peak-season advanced courses start around ¥85,000 (roughly NTD17,850). HPC Ski School's training camps and advanced courses are led by a former Japan Olympic team coach, taught fully in Chinese with real-time correction on the slope.

Do beginners really need a coach? Can you just teach yourself?

If your goal is just photos and enjoying the scenery, you probably don't need a coach. But if you actually want to learn to ski, a coach's real-time video review and hands-on demonstration make it much clearer which part of your form is off and how to correct it — something that's hard to replicate from travel blogs or casual pointers from friends. Because a native language is always the most comfortable to learn in, especially for complete beginners, lessons taught in Chinese tend to be the most effective, and they also make it easier to communicate clearly in an emergency.

How much do Chinese-speaking coaches cost? What's the difference between half-day and full-day?

Pricing for Chinese-speaking coaches varies by coach level, resort, and group size. As an example, a Level 1 coach runs around NTD16,000 for a full day and NTD11,500 for a half day, while a Level 2 coach runs around NTD18,000 full day and NTD13,500 half day. Another common pricing structure is by student count: one coach with one student runs about NTD14,500 full day / NTD11,500 half day; one coach with two students about NTD15,700 full day / NTD12,700 half day; and one coach with three students about NTD16,900 full day / NTD13,900 half day. Overall, half-day lessons (roughly 2-3 hours) start around ¥10,000, full-day lessons (about 6 hours) run close to double that, and peak season (January-February) can add another 20% or more at some resorts.

How much do advanced or terrain park courses cost, and who are they for?

Skiers who already carry parallel turns and want to sharpen high-speed carving or take on terrain park features (jumps, boxes) typically sign up for advanced or park courses. These demand more specialized coaching, which pushes the price up. At one resort ski school's peak-season pricing, advanced courses start around ¥85,000 (roughly NTD17,850) — nearly double the ¥40,000 (roughly NTD8,400) starting price for beginner courses, largely reflecting the limited availability of advanced-level coaches and a higher ratio of one-on-one instruction. If you're planning an advanced course or training camp, booking 1-2 months ahead is a good idea to avoid coach schedules filling up during peak season (January-February).

How should you care for your snowboard after the season ends, and is storing your gear in Hokkaido worth it?

How often should you wax a snowboard, and how do you store it after the season ends?

There's no fixed schedule for waxing — it depends on how often you ride. You can judge by feel (does the board glide as smoothly as before?), by checking whether the base looks dull under good light, or with a water-drop test (if drops sit on the base instead of sliding off, it's time to wax). A common rule of thumb is to wax every 3-5 days of riding to keep the base in top condition. Once the season is fully over, apply a "seal wax" before storing the board to protect the base from moisture and oxidation, which preserves both the glide feel and the lifespan of the board for next season. If you ride more than 20 days a year, buying your own tools and learning hot-waxing is usually more cost-effective; if you ride fewer than 10 days a year, it's simpler to have a shop wax it for you (roughly 2,000-3,000 yen per session) or use the tune-up service bundled with a storage plan.

What's the actual difference in teaching style between group and private ski camp classes?

Private lessons can be booked for 1-6 people, with no unfamiliar students mixed in -- the coach first assesses your current level and the specific movements you want to work on, then decides the actual teaching content and pace for the day, making it fully customized. Snowboard maintenance: how often should you wax, and how do you know when it's time?

How do you DIY wax and tune edges at home?

The basic DIY waxing process is: melt the wax block against a hot iron so it drips evenly onto the base, then run the iron over the dripped wax to spread it evenly across the entire base, scrape off the excess with a wax scraper, and finally buff the surface with a soft cloth, paper towel or cork block so the wax bonds smoothly with the base. For edge tuning, run a fingertip along the edge after every session to check for damage; if you feel an obvious burr or roughness, the edge has dulled from impacts or friction and needs a tuning tool — otherwise your board's turning and grip precision will noticeably deteriorate.

How Do You Learn Ski Park Courses? How Many Days Before You Can Try a Box or Rail?

Park features like boxes and rails are graded beginner, intermediate, and advanced, and the most basic features only require being able to make smooth S-turns on a green run. That said, a full beginner course shouldn't be shorter than 2 days — that's what it takes to lock in balance and stopping fundamentals so your technique doesn't stall halfway through.

How do beginners start with park boxes and rails?

Jibbing features in the park (a general term for boxes, rails, and similar terrain features you slide onto and across) are graded by difficulty from beginner to advanced. Most riders start with the basic 50-50 — riding straight across a feature with even weight on both feet. Once you're comfortable riding straight onto a feature, you progress to jumping on from the side with a frontside or backside 50-50 — a gradual progression that reduces injury risk.

  • Entry requirement: able to make smooth S-turns on a green run
  • First move to practice: 50-50 (balanced, flat-footed ride across a feature)
  • Next progression: frontside 50-50, backside 50-50 (jumping on from the side)

How many days of lessons do beginners actually need?

A full ski or snowboard beginner course shouldn't run shorter than 2 days: day one usually covers basic balance, stopping, and turning, while day two reinforces what you learned and builds toward smoother, connected turns. A single day of lessons often isn't enough to lock in the fundamentals — meaning you may end up re-learning basics the next time you ski, which costs both extra time and money.

Can you learn skiing and snowboarding in the same lesson?

We don't recommend it. Skiing and snowboarding rely on completely different weight transfer and edge control mechanics, and mixing both in a single lesson tends to make the two techniques interfere with each other, slowing your progress. If you already have a base level and want to add a park-focused lesson, confirm your instructor has experience coaching students in the park, and book a course that matches whichever discipline — skis or snowboard — you're already comfortable on.
